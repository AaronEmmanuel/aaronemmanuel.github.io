How the project developed

Start with a working robot.
Then give it eyes and words.

01

Build a dependable baseline

First, make the arm pick and place.

I built the workcell and a scripted controller so the gripper had to lift simulated parts through finger contact. That gave the project a working reference before adding AI.

02

Learn from an approach that failed

Copying movements was not enough.

A model trained to imitate demonstrations struggled to complete tasks. I kept those failed experiments and changed direction: a learned model now helps calculate robot movement, while feedback checks the arm’s progress.

03

Connect language to what the camera sees

Ask for a part. Check the answer.

A vision-language model sees the camera image and request together. Separate checks verify its proposed target, and depth sensing estimates where to grasp. Missing or ambiguous parts lead to clarification.

04

Handle the moments that go wrong

Success needs more than reaching the tray.

The software checks the grasp and placement, allows one retry after a missed grasp, and stops when the target moves or feedback fails. Testing exposed waiting and timing problems that shaped the final controller.

The project connects an everyday request to a visible, checked robot action. It demonstrates the engineering behind an AI-enabled workcell, from a conventional baseline to camera-guided manipulation.

What still needs work

The scene has three known parts, two fixed trays and a calibrated camera. Arbitrary objects, clutter and a physical robot need further development and testing. The simulation runs slower than real time, and its Stop button is not a hardware emergency stop.

My contribution

I developed the workcell, control, perception, learning pipeline, ROS interfaces and evaluation with AI-assisted coding. The Panda robot assets come from Google DeepMind’s MuJoCo Menagerie; Qwen provides the pretrained vision-language model.

A closer look at the engineering Vision, learning and robot control ↓

From words to a checked target

Qwen3-VL sees the request and a numbered camera image. Independent colour and geometry checks validate its proposed target, and calibrated RGB-D sensing locates the grasp. An unclear or missing part leads to clarification rather than an arbitrary pick.

From a target to movement

A PyTorch model learns the robot’s kinematics from 60,000 synthetic samples. Conventional feedback uses its predicted Jacobian to calculate joint movement. The task sequence and gripper control remain engineered; the vision-language model does not directly drive the joints.

From movement to a result

ROS 2 connects the camera, joint feedback, controller and MuJoCo simulation on Ubuntu. Contact, progress and placement checks determine completion. The final planned simulation evaluation passed 10/10 placement cases and 15/15 checks overall, including clarification and stopping behavior.

The scene uses known parts and calibrated simulated sensors. Continuous-operation timing still needs further qualification before broader deployment. Foundations: Python, ROS 2 Humble, MuJoCo, PyTorch, Qwen3-VL and llama.cpp.
